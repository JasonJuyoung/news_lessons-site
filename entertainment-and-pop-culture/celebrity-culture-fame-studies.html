The World Cup as Spectacle: Fame, Politics, and Economics After the Final Whistle

Key definitions

  • Halo effect — A phenomenon in fame studies where association with a high-visibility event or figure increases a person's or brand's perceived status and public attention, independent of their direct contribution.
  • Sports mega-event — A large-scale sporting competition, such as the World Cup, that generates substantial media coverage, economic activity, and infrastructure investment for host regions.
  • Host city legacy — The lasting economic, infrastructural, and reputational effects that remain in a city or region after a major event concludes, including tourism revenue and security capabilities.
  • Media spectacle — An event staged or covered in a way that prioritizes dramatic visual and emotional impact for mass audiences, often blending entertainment, politics, and commerce.
  • Red card — A disciplinary sanction in soccer that results in a player's immediate ejection from the match for serious foul play or misconduct.

Explanation

Mega sporting events like the World Cup function simultaneously as athletic competitions and cultural spectacles, drawing celebrities, politicians, and media figures into their orbit regardless of their direct role in the sport. The scrutiny applied to figures like Robbie Williams and the controversy over the Paredes red card ruling illustrate how public attention during these events extends well beyond the field of play, turning incidental moments into viral controversies that shape public perception long after the final whistle. This is a core concern of fame studies: visibility during a mega-event can attach itself to individuals in ways that outlast the event itself, for better or worse.

The political dimension seen in commentary about Trump's travel-related decisions and the criticized photograph reflects how heads of state often seek association with globally popular events to bolster public image, while critics use the same imagery to question motives or optics. Sports mega-events thus become contested symbolic terrain, where political actors, media commentators, and fans negotiate meaning simultaneously.

Finally, the economic and logistical afterlife of the tournament—parade attendance figures, host-city financial tallies, security infrastructure improvements, and proposals to expand future tournaments to 64 teams—demonstrates why these events matter beyond entertainment. Host cities and federations treat World Cups as long-term investments, using the tournament's cultural momentum to justify infrastructure spending, security upgrades, and future format expansions intended to capture even larger global audiences.
