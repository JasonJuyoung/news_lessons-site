Diplomatic Rupture Over a US-Backed Gaza Peace Plan

Security, Conflict & Public Safety: War · Armed Conflict Studies
Secondary tags: Geopolitics · International Relations, Diplomacy
2026/08/10 (KST)

Summary

Israeli security services publicly acknowledged for the first time a dedicated operation by the IDF and Shin Bet to track down and kill those responsible for the October 7 attacks, revealing a military and intelligence campaign running in parallel with diplomatic efforts. The disclosure came as the diplomatic track hardened: the government led by Prime Minister Netanyahu rejected a specific 15-point plan backed by the Trump administration, which paired the Hamas disarmament sought in earlier proposals with a further requirement of Israeli withdrawal from Gaza — turning previously reported friction over postwar governance terms into an open rupture between Washington and Jerusalem. The conflict on the ground continued throughout, with rescue crews recovering nineteen bodies from the rubble of a destroyed building in Gaza.

Key definitions

  • Disarmament — The reduction or elimination of a group's or state's weapons and military capacity, often demanded as a precondition in ceasefire or peace negotiations.
  • Withdrawal (military) — The removal of armed forces from an occupied or contested territory, typically as part of a ceasefire, peace agreement, or de-escalation plan.
  • Shin Bet — Israel's internal security service, responsible for counterintelligence, counterterrorism, and protective security within Israel and the occupied territories.
  • IDF (Israel Defense Forces) — The military forces of the State of Israel, encompassing land, air, and naval branches, responsible for national defense and military operations.
  • Ceasefire plan — A proposed or negotiated framework intended to halt active hostilities between warring parties, often including conditions such as troop withdrawals, prisoner exchanges, or disarmament.

Explanation

The rejection of a US-brokered plan by a close ally illustrates the limits of external diplomatic leverage even from a country's most significant international patron. Trump's 15-point proposal reportedly sought a negotiated endgame combining Hamas's disarmament with an Israeli military withdrawal, terms that Netanyahu's government judged incompatible with its security objectives, exposing a gap between US preferences for de-escalation and Israeli assessments of what conditions are necessary before withdrawing forces. This kind of rejection is significant because it signals that a wartime ally will not automatically defer to the diplomatic initiatives of even its principal military and political backer, complicating US efforts to broker regional stability. The continued discovery of casualties, such as the nineteen bodies recovered from a collapsed building, underscores the human cost that persists regardless of diplomatic maneuvering, while the acknowledgment of a targeted Shin Bet–IDF campaign to hunt down October 7 perpetrators reveals a long-running, deliberate component of Israel's response that operates independently of, and potentially in tension with, ceasefire diplomacy. Together these developments show how military operations, intelligence campaigns, and great-power diplomacy interact—and sometimes conflict—in a protracted conflict where each actor pursues distinct and not fully aligned objectives.

The Axis of Resistance and the Strain of Sustained Confrontation

Security, Conflict & Public Safety: War · Armed Conflict Studies
Secondary tags: Geopolitics · International Relations, Diplomacy
2026/08/06 (KST)

Summary

Recent reporting highlights three interlocking dynamics in the Middle East's ongoing confrontation between the United States, Israel, and Iran-aligned forces. Foreign Affairs traces how the so-called Axis of Resistance—the network of Iran-backed groups—has rebuilt capacity after significant setbacks. The Times of Israel reports that Prime Minister Netanyahu has publicly rejected certain terms proposed by an international 'Board of Peace' mechanism for disarming Hamas, revealing friction over how postwar Gaza governance and demilitarization should proceed. Meanwhile, CBS News reports that the United States has expended nearly its entire stockpile of long-range missiles during its military engagement with Iran, raising questions about the sustainability of continued American involvement in the region's escalating conflicts.

Key definitions

  • Axis of Resistance — An informal network of Iran-aligned state and non-state actors, including groups in Lebanon, Iraq, Yemen, and the Palestinian territories, that coordinate to varying degrees against the United States and Israel.
  • Board of Peace — A proposed or established international mechanism intended to oversee arrangements in Gaza, including terms related to the disarmament of Hamas as part of a broader peace or ceasefire framework.
  • Disarmament (in conflict resolution) — The process of removing or decommissioning the weapons and military capabilities of an armed group, typically as a condition for ending hostilities or transitioning to governance.
  • Munitions stockpile attrition — The depletion of a military's stored supply of weapons systems, such as long-range missiles, through sustained use in combat operations, which can limit future operational capacity.
  • Deterrence — A strategic posture aimed at preventing an adversary from taking hostile action by maintaining the credible capability and will to inflict unacceptable costs in response.

Explanation

The durability of the Axis of Resistance despite repeated military and political blows illustrates a core feature of asymmetric, networked adversaries: decentralization allows for regeneration. Rather than depending on a single command structure, the network's constituent groups can absorb losses to one node while others continue operating, and external patronage—chiefly from Iran—provides the resources and training needed for recovery. This resilience complicates any strategy premised on quickly and permanently degrading such a network through military strikes alone, since organizational recovery can outpace the tempo of punitive action.

The dispute over Hamas disarmament terms under a proposed Board of Peace framework reflects a deeper structural problem in ending prolonged conflicts: the gap between military outcomes and durable political settlements. Even where a ceasefire or peace framework is on the table, disagreement over verification, sequencing, and enforcement of disarmament provisions can stall implementation. Netanyahu's public rejection of specific terms signals that Israel views certain disarmament conditions as insufficient to guarantee its security objectives, underscoring how postwar governance arrangements are often as contested as the fighting itself.

The near-exhaustion of the U.S. long-range missile stockpile after engagement with Iran points to a resource constraint that shapes strategic options regardless of political will. Modern precision-guided munitions are expensive and slow to manufacture, meaning that sustained high-intensity operations can outpace production and replenishment. This creates a feedback loop relevant to deterrence theory: if an adversary or observer perceives that a military's magazine depth is limited, the credibility of future threats to use force may erode, potentially inviting further testing of resolve. Together, these three threads—organizational resilience of non-state networks, unresolved terms for ending active conflicts, and the material limits of sustained military engagement—demonstrate why Middle East escalation dynamics resist simple military or diplomatic fixes and instead require attention to networks, institutions, and logistics simultaneously.

Iran, Hormuz, and the Oil Shock

Security, Conflict & Public Safety: War · Armed Conflict Studies
Secondary tags: Energy Systems, Geopolitics · International Relations, Finance · Markets
2026/07/09 (KST)

Summary

A fragile U.S.–Iran ceasefire has collapsed. President Trump declared it "over," escalated his rhetoric against Iranian leaders, and ordered a new round of strikes; the two sides have traded further attacks. Attention has quickly turned to the Strait of Hormuz, with reporting that Washington is bracing for a fight over the waterway. Financial markets moved sharply on the news: oil prices jumped, U.S. stock futures fell, and 10-year Treasury yields climbed as investors weighed renewed inflation risk from higher energy costs.

Key definitions

  • Ceasefire — a negotiated pause in fighting; unlike a peace treaty, it is fragile and easily reversed, as here.
  • Chokepoint — a narrow, geographically fixed passage through which a large share of global trade or energy must pass, with no easy detour. The Strait of Hormuz — through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil moves — is the classic example.
  • Oil price shock — a sudden spike in crude prices, often triggered by supply fears, that ripples through the wider economy.
  • Inflation transmission — when conflict raises energy prices, it feeds into general inflation, which pushes up bond yields as investors demand more return.

Explanation

This episode is a textbook illustration of how a military conflict transmits into the global economy through energy. The mechanism runs through a chokepoint: because so much of the world's oil physically passes through the Strait of Hormuz, any credible threat to that passage raises the expected price of oil everywhere — regardless of whether a single barrel is actually stopped. Markets price the risk, not just the event.

From there the shock propagates. Higher oil prices raise the cost of nearly everything that must be produced or transported, stoking inflation fears; investors respond by demanding higher yields on bonds, and equities fall as the outlook darkens. This is why a distant military exchange can move a retiree's portfolio within hours. The situation also shows the logic of coercive leverage over a chokepoint: control (or the threat of disruption) at Hormuz confers strategic power far beyond the immediate battlefield — the same dynamic, in a different theater, that makes energy infrastructure a recurring target in modern conflict.

Ukraine's Deep-Strike Campaign

Security, Conflict & Public Safety: War · Armed Conflict Studies
Secondary tags: Energy Systems, Geopolitics · International Relations, Economics (Macro · Micro)
2026/07/07 (KST)

Summary

Ukraine has intensified long-range drone strikes reaching deep inside Russian territory, and the target set is increasingly economic. Ukrainian drones struck Russia's largest oil refinery in one of the deepest strikes of the war, and reporting indicates that nearly every Russian region has felt a resulting fuel crisis as attacks on refining and distribution mount. The Institute for the Study of War assessed that these strike campaigns are likely to keep degrading both Russia's economy and its military operations in Ukraine. Meanwhile the war's other direction continues: Russian missile and drone barrages killed at least 22 people in Ukraine, even as Ukraine has reportedly remade its air defenses and Russia has adapted its tactics in response. Together the reports describe a war fought not only at the front line but across each side's rear-area infrastructure.

Key definitions

  • Deep strike — an attack aimed well beyond the front line, at targets in an adversary's interior such as factories, refineries, and logistics hubs.
  • Energy infrastructure — the refineries, pipelines, storage, and power facilities that turn raw resources into usable fuel and electricity; a dual-use target supporting both civilian life and the war economy.
  • Economic warfare (cost-imposition) — strategies designed to raise the economic price of continuing a war, rather than to seize territory directly.
  • Attrition — winning by steadily wearing down an opponent's resources, forces, or will over time.
  • Air defense — the radars, interceptors, and electronic-warfare systems used to detect and destroy incoming aircraft, missiles, and drones.

Explanation

Wars are won not only where armies meet but where their strength is manufactured and fueled. Strategists have long argued that a modern military is only as strong as the logistics and economy behind it — an insight running from Clausewitz's attention to a nation's war-making capacity to twentieth-century theories of strategic bombing. Ukraine's focus on Russian refineries reflects this logic directly: Russia is a petrostate, and refined fuel powers both its armored forces and its civilian economy and export revenue. Striking refining capacity therefore attacks three things at once — the battlefield's fuel supply, the treasury that funds the war, and the domestic normalcy that sustains public morale.

This is a form of cost-imposition: unable to match Russia in sheer mass, Ukraine uses relatively cheap drones to force expensive, widespread disruption — a favorable exchange of costs. The episode also illustrates the endless measure–countermeasure cycle of warfare: as Ukraine rebuilt its air defenses, Russia changed the timing, mix, and routes of its attacks, and each adaptation invites the next. For a student of armed conflict, this is a compact case study in how contemporary wars blur the line between "military" and "civilian/economic" targets — and why energy systems have become central terrain.

  • RAND Corporation — public research on strategic interdiction and targeting of war economies (rand.org).
  • Carl von Clausewitz, On War — the classic on the link between war, economy, and national will; widely available in summary and in strategy syllabi.
